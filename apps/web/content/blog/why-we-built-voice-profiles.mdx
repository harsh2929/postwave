When we started talking to writers about adding AI to a newsletter tool,
the same sentence kept coming up: **"I don't want it to sound like AI."**

Not "I don't want AI to do the writing" — most writers we talked to were
fine with AI handling the boring 60% of a sequence (the welcome flow, the
re-engagement, the "here's what we shipped this month" recap). The
concern was specifically about the output being recognizably,
embarrassingly AI-flavored. The em-dashes. The "In today's fast-paced
world." The earnest summary paragraph at the end.

It turns out that when you read a writer's actual past newsletter and
compare it to what a base GPT-class model produces from a one-line brief,
the gap isn't subtle. Voice — vocabulary, rhythm, structure, the things
the writer never repeats and the things they always repeat — is doing a
lot of the work we don't usually credit it for. Strip it out and the
content is generic; strip it out at the start of a six-email sequence and
your readers can tell by email two.

## The before-and-after that convinced us

Here's a real example from a beta. The writer is a software founder
running a weekly engineering newsletter. We asked for a re-engagement
email to subscribers who hadn't opened in 60 days.

**Base model, no voice profile:**

> Subject: We miss you!
>
> Hi [First Name],
>
> We've noticed you haven't been opening our emails lately, and we wanted
> to check in. Our newsletter is full of valuable insights about
> engineering and we'd hate for you to miss out.
>
> If you're still interested, just click the button below to stay
> subscribed. If not, no hard feelings — we'll remove you from the list.
>
> [Stay subscribed]
>
> Looking forward to hearing from you!

That's not bad in any single line. It's bad as a whole because it
sounds like nobody you know. The CTA is a generic button. The
subject line is a cliché. There's no specificity, no in-jokes, nothing
that even hints at what the newsletter is actually about.

**Same brief, with the writer's voice profile applied:**

> Subject: are you still in?
>
> Hey, I noticed you haven't opened the last few. That's fine — inboxes
> are landfills and most newsletters don't earn the click. I'm not going
> to pretend mine does either, but I'd rather know than keep showing up
> in your spam folder pretending we're still on speaking terms.
>
> So: one button to stay, one to leave. No guilt either way. If you stay,
> next week's is the long-overdue write-up of the time we accidentally
> mailed 200K customers from a staging environment, which is a story
> that's been waiting for its moment.
>
> — Sam

The second one isn't better because it's longer or shorter. It's better
because it sounds like Sam. The cadence ("inboxes are landfills",
"earn the click", "speaking terms"), the specific story tease, the
sign-off as a single em-dash and a name — those are stylistic features
the voice profile picked up from 18 months of his back catalog.

The model didn't write that. The model rewrote a base draft to match the
profile, and a fidelity scorer then approved it.

## What's in a voice profile

A Moosewave voice profile is built from up to five sources:

- **Imported newsletter archive.** Pulled from the migration, every past
  campaign with body text.
- **Twitter / X.** Up to 1,000 most recent posts, threads expanded.
- **Blog RSS.** The writer's primary blog if they have one.
- **Podcast transcripts.** If they upload them — many founders do.
- **Pasted samples.** A "voice samples" textarea for anyone who has
  good writing somewhere we can't crawl.

From those, we extract four orthogonal feature sets:

1. **Stylistic features.** Average sentence length, em-dash frequency,
   parenthetical density, exclamation rate, contraction rate, second
   vs. first person ratio, presence of hedging phrases, sign-off style.
2. **Vocabulary fingerprint.** A TF-IDF distribution over the writer's
   vocabulary normalized against a general-English baseline. Captures
   words used disproportionately *and* common words deliberately
   avoided.
3. **Structural patterns.** Opening conventions, list usage, quote
   blocks, image cadence, section break style. How they get from "hello"
   to "here's the thing" to "here's the ask".
4. **Tonal vector.** A 768-dim embedding of voice samples, used as the
   target for cosine-similarity matching.

These aren't all weighted equally. The tonal vector dominates the
fidelity score, but stylistic features catch the cases where the model
produces semantically-similar prose in the wrong register — formally
correct but missing the contractions and the sign-off.

## The fidelity score

Every email Voice AI generates gets scored from 0 to 100. The score is
a weighted combination:

```
fidelity = 0.45 * cosine(generated_embedding, profile_tonal_vector)
         + 0.20 * stylistic_feature_match
         + 0.20 * vocabulary_alignment
         + 0.15 * structural_fit
```

(Weights are tuned per-profile from a small calibration set the writer
approves at onboarding. We default to those numbers.)

Below 60 we **auto-regenerate**. The writer never sees the bad draft —
they see the email at the moment it crosses 60. Above 80 we mark it
high-fidelity in the editor; the writer can ship as-is or tweak. Between
60 and 80 we show the score next to the draft so the writer can decide
whether it's worth a manual pass.

This is the part that took the longest to get right. We tried hard
thresholds (≥80 or it doesn't ship), and we tried no thresholds (always
show, always editable). Hard thresholds frustrate writers because some
emails legitimately need to sound less like them — a formal corporate
update from an otherwise casual writer, for instance. No threshold
defeats the point. The auto-regenerate-below-60 setting plus the
visible-score-above-60 setting was where the beta cohort consistently
landed.

## Why we built this instead of "AI subject line generator"

There's a cheaper version of "AI for newsletters" that gives you a
subject-line generator and a "rewrite this paragraph" button. We
shipped neither in V1, deliberately.

The reason: those features are commodity. Every editor has them now.
They're not why anyone would switch newsletter platforms. The reason a
writer might switch platforms specifically because of AI is if the AI
saves them from writing 80% of a sequence they don't want to write — a
welcome series, a course launch, a book pre-order — *and* the saved
draft is good enough to send without an embarrassed apology to long-time
readers.

That's a much higher bar. It's also where the leverage is. A subject-line
generator saves a writer 30 seconds. A welcome sequence in their actual
voice saves them six hours and a weekend they'd been putting off for
four months. We'd rather build the second thing properly than a dozen of
the first thing badly.

## What the writer sees

In the sequence editor, every email in a Voice AI-generated sequence has
a small fidelity badge in the top-right corner. Click it and you get the
breakdown:

- The four sub-scores (tonal, stylistic, vocabulary, structural).
- The two stylistic features that scored worst on this draft.
- A "regenerate with hint" button if you want to push toward a specific
  feeling without rewriting the whole thing.

We resisted the temptation to hide the score. Visible scoring makes the
writer the judge — which is the right relationship. The model is a
collaborator, not an oracle.

## What's next

The piece we haven't shipped yet is **profile drift detection.** A
writer's voice changes over months and years. A profile trained on
their 2024 archive will gradually drift from their 2026 voice as they
grow. We're working on a continuous re-training that takes their actual
sent campaigns (the ones they shipped, not the ones they regenerated)
and uses them to nudge the profile forward over time.

That's the thing nobody else is going to build for you, because it's
specific to a writing tool and it's not where the easy demo lives.
